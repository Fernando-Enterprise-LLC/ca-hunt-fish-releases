Privacy Policy

CA Hunt & Fish Guide — effective September 24, 2026

The short version

CA Hunt & Fish Guide is published by Fernando Enterprise LLC, a California limited liability company. The app has no accounts, no analytics, no advertising, and no trackers of any kind. There is no server behind it. The developer receives no personal information about you, holds none, sells none, and shares none.

Everything you enter into the app — your licence and tag records, your dive log, your equipment list, your waypoints, your watched places, your settings — is written to a file inside the app on your own device. The app never sends any of it anywhere, and the developer never receives it, with one exception spelled out in section 4: asking for Area information at a waypoint or a pin sends that point's coordinate to the National Weather Service for the forecast there, and nothing else. One thing does leave your phone, and it is not the app doing it: if you back up your iPhone, iOS puts these files in that backup, the same as it does for other apps. That is between you and Apple, and section 2 says exactly what is known about it.

The app does make requests over the internet, and this policy says exactly which ones, to whom, and what they carry. Section 4 is the important one, and it is more detailed than most privacy policies because the honest answer needs the detail.

1. What the developer collects about you

Nothing.

There is no sign-up, no login, no email list, no crash reporting, no analytics SDK, no advertising SDK, no advertising identifier, no fingerprinting, and no server that could receive your data even if the app tried to send it. The developer cannot identify you, cannot tell how many times you opened the app, cannot tell what you looked at, and cannot connect anything in this app to you.

Because nothing is collected, there is nothing to sell, nothing to share, and nothing to disclose to anyone for their direct marketing purposes — and the developer does none of those things.

2. What is stored on your device, itemised

All of the following is written inside the app's own container on your device — most of it as files in Application Support, and the preference-shaped items in the app's own preferences store. The app sends none of it to the developer or to anyone else, and there is no server it could be sent to. All of it is removed when you delete the app. The one exception is an iPhone backup, described at the end of this section.

  • Your wallet. The documents you tell the app you hold: licences, validations, stamps, report cards, and tags. For each one, whatever you chose to enter — the document number, the licence year or season year, purchase and printed valid-through dates, harvest and report-filed dates, a title you gave it, and your own notes. Also your GO ID if you enter one, your hunter-education proof (which kind, and the detail you typed), and any course records you add, including certificate numbers.
  • Your dive log. Each entry's date, the spot, maximum depth, the depth at which you saw fish, species seen, species taken, visibility, water temperature, a tide note, and your own notes.
  • Your equipment list. The catalogue identifiers of the items you say you own, plus any nickname you give them and the date you added them. The app deliberately stores no free-text ballistics of any kind.
  • Your waypoints. A name, a note, and the coordinates you saved.
  • Your watched places. Which lakes, zones or places you are watching, their names, and when you added them.
  • Your saved trips. For each trip you save: the spot you planned it around, the dates you chose, how far around the spot you asked the app to look, and the plan exactly as it read when you saved it — the same text the app's "Copy this plan" writes out, with the sources and read times it carried then. The spot is a place from this app's own list, or the pin you dropped or pasted — and where it is a pin, the pin's coordinates are in that file, because a saved trip that had forgotten which spot it was about could not be run again. Deleting the trip deletes them with it. Trips saved by older versions of the app also hold the activity, the conditions you set and the numbers you set them to, and the result of that run; the app no longer asks for conditions and says so on the trip's own file. All of it is saved on your device, in the app's own files, like everything else in this list. Re-running a saved trip keeps the text you saved until you choose to keep the new one. The developer never receives a saved trip, and there is no account to attach one to.
  • Your settings and profile. Residency, interests, which activities your checklist covers, the onboarding flag, and your map layer preferences.
  • Your acceptance of the Terms of Use. Which version you accepted, when, and which app version you accepted it in.
  • The regulations dataset itself, downloaded from the app's file host, and the app's own record of it: which version you have, which update channel it came from, when the app last checked for a new one and when a check last failed, and which version your reminders were last scheduled against.
  • Whether you are a founding user. The app asks Apple, once, for its signed record of when your Apple Account first obtained this app, and keeps the answer — yes or no, and that date — so the question does not have to be asked again and so it still works offline. That record comes from Apple to your device; it is not sent anywhere, and the developer never sees it.
  • Which subscription you have already been shown the terms for. If you subscribe, the app keeps Apple's identifier for that subscription so it shows you the summary of what you bought, and how to cancel it, once rather than after every renewal. It is Apple's identifier for the purchase, not for you, and it is not sent anywhere.
  • Map tiles you have looked at. When a topographic or nautical layer is switched on, the tile images downloaded for the area on your screen are kept in the app's cache folder so the map still draws where there is no signal. That cache is a record, on your own phone, of which parts of the map you have viewed. iOS may clear it at any time, deleting the app clears it, and nothing about it is sent anywhere.
  • Depth contours you have looked at. With the Depth overlay on, the contour lines and depth areas NOAA returned for the areas on your screen are held by the app so panning back over water you have already looked at does not ask NOAA again. Like the tile cache above, that is a record, on your own phone, of which stretches of coast you have looked at. It is held only while the app is running — closing the app discards it — and nothing about it is sent anywhere.
  • The counties' beach water-quality readings, as this app last received them. When you switch the Water quality layer on, the app downloads one file from its own file host (beach_water_quality_current.json) and keeps it, with a small companion file describing it, as beach_water_quality_live.json and beach_water_quality_live_meta.json. It is the same file for every reader — the readings seven California coastal counties published, in those counties' own words, collected by the developer and not by your phone — so it says nothing about you, nothing about which beach you looked at, and nothing about where you are. It is kept so that the layer still works where there is no signal, and so that opening it a second time does not download it again. It is replaced when a newer one is published, and deleting the app deletes it.
  • This app's own record of when it last checked its sources. Once a day, this app's publisher re-reads the agency pages and documents the app's data comes from and writes down what it found — the address it read, when it read it, and whether the page had changed. The app downloads that record from its own file host (verification_current.json) and keeps it, with a small companion file describing it, as verification_live.json and verification_live_meta.json. It is the same file for every reader — it holds no regulation, says nothing about you, and names nothing you looked at — so the request carries no query string, no body and no cookies. It is kept so the home screen still knows when the sources were last checked where there is no signal. That host is the same one § 4 already names under "Dataset downloads"; this adds no new destination. It is replaced when a newer one is published, and deleting the app deletes it.
  • The State's own bacteria readings: none, and the old ones are still deleted. The app used to keep the State's results for the monitoring stations on your screen in its own file (beach_water_quality_cache.json), together with when each station was last asked about, and it used to keep a county-status sidecar beside it. It keeps neither, and the first launch of every version since deletes all three (beach_water_quality_cache.json, beach_status_live.json, beach_status_live_meta.json) if they are on your phone, so that this sentence is true of your phone and not only of a new one. The file described in the bullet above is a different file, with a different name, holding the counties' own published words rather than the State's measurements.

Backups

If you back up your iPhone to iCloud or to a computer, iOS decides what goes into that backup, and these files live in the app's Application Support folder, which iOS ordinarily includes. That backup is between you and Apple under Apple's terms. The developer does not control it, never sees it, and cannot read it.

3. Your records are yours

Everything you entered can be viewed, edited, and deleted inside the app, on the screen where it was created — the wallet, the dive log, the equipment list, the waypoint list, the watchlist, and Settings. Deleting a record deletes it from the file on your device.

Five of the items in section 2 are the app's own bookkeeping rather than something you entered, and they have no delete button of their own: the record of which version of the Terms you accepted and when, the app's record of the dataset, whether you are a founding user, which subscription you have already been shown the terms for, and the cached map tiles. The first four exist so the app behaves correctly and the fifth so the map draws offline; none is sent anywhere, and all five go when the app is deleted.

Deleting the app removes everything at once. There is no copy on any server, so there is nothing to ask the developer to delete and no request process to go through: the delete button in the app, and deleting the app, are the whole mechanism.

4. Location, precisely

Your GPS position stays on your phone. When you use "Where am I?", the location fix is compared against boundary data already stored on the device, and the answer is computed there. The fix is not sent to the developer, to any server, or to anyone else. There is no code path in the app that sends your device's position over the network.

What does leave the device is the places you look at, not where you are. These are two different things, and the old version of this policy blurred them. Specifically:

  • Map tiles. When you pan or zoom the map with a topographic or nautical layer switched on, the app asks the U.S. Geological Survey (basemap.nationalmap.gov) or NOAA (encdirect.noaa.gov) for the tiles covering the area on your screen. Those servers therefore see which part of California you are looking at. Tiles already fetched are kept in the app's cache on your device, so the same area asked for again may not produce a request at all. And, with the Depth overlay on, the app asks that same NOAA service for the depth contours covering the area on your screen. That request carries the corner coordinates of your screen, so that server sees which stretch of coast you are looking at — the same shape of disclosure as the tiles themselves. It is made when the map comes to rest, not while you are moving it, and not at all when you are zoomed out past the point where the app draws depth.
  • The standard base map is Apple's, drawn by MapKit, which is part of iOS. When Apple's base map is displayed, Apple receives the map region being drawn, under Apple's privacy policy, the same as in any other app that shows a map.
  • Elevation. When you press and hold a point on the map, the coordinate readout opens and automatically asks the USGS elevation service (epqs.nationalmap.gov) for that point's elevation. You do not have to tap anything more for that request to go out, so it is worth being plain about: opening the readout sends that coordinate. It is a point you chose on the map, not a reading of where you are — but if you press where you are standing, it is the same point, and you should know that. Opening a waypoint you saved earlier does not send anything; the elevation request happens only on the readout for a point you just pressed.
  • Depth. When the Depth overlay is switched on and you press and hold a point on the map, the coordinate readout also asks NOAA's ENC Direct service (encdirect.noaa.gov) what depth its nautical charts give for that point. Like the elevation request above, it goes out automatically when the readout opens — you do not have to tap anything more — so that server sees the point you pressed. It is a point you chose on the map, not a reading of where you are, but if you press where you are standing it is the same point. When the Depth overlay is off, the app makes no depth request at all, and the readout shows no depth row. Opening a waypoint you saved earlier sends nothing. That service is already named above for the chart tiles; the Depth overlay adds no new host.
  • Weather, tides, buoys and ocean data. These are fetched for coordinates and station identifiers that the app already publishes in its dataset — a lake, a zone, a named tide station, a named buoy — not for your position. They go to the National Weather Service (api.weather.gov), NOAA tides and currents (api.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov) and the National Data Buoy Center (www.ndbc.noaa.gov). Area information is different: when you open or refresh it for a pin you dropped, coordinates you entered or a waypoint you saved, and that point is in California or off its coast, the app sends the point's coordinate to the National Weather Service (api.weather.gov) for the forecast and the nearest weather station's readings there, and nothing else — the rest of the page is worked out on your phone, and a waypoint's name and note stay on the device.
  • The wave buoys Scripps runs: one request, and it is the same one for everybody. When you open a spear spot, the app asks Scripps Institution of Oceanography's data server (thredds.cdip.ucsd.edu) for the last three hours of wave height, wave period, wave direction and sea-surface temperature at every one of its buoys at once. It does not ask about the buoy nearest you, and it could not: the request names no buoy, no spot and no coordinate, and it is byte-identical for every reader of this app anywhere in California. That server therefore learns that this app asked, and nothing else — not which spot you opened, not which stretch of coast you were looking at. The app then picks the buoy for your spot out of the answer, on your phone. Asking for one buoy at a time would have been faster to write and would have told them which one you wanted, which is why it is not what happens.
  • The pier instrument nearest a spot: one request, and this one does name the pier. Many spear spots have a pier or wharf within reach carrying an instrument that measures how cloudy the water is, run by California's ocean observing systems (CeNCOOS and SCCOOS). When you open such a spot, the app asks their data server (erddap.caloos.org, or erddap.cencoos.org for a few stations) for the last two days of readings at that one station. Unlike the buoy request above, this one names a station, so that server sees which pier's readings you asked for — the same shape of disclosure as the tide station and the buoy number in the paragraph above this section's satellite ones, and for the same reason: it is the only way to ask. It is a pier this app publishes in its own dataset, not a reading of where you are, and the app always shows you how far that pier is from the spot — two kilometres at some, forty at others — because the same number means two different things at those two distances. A spot with no such instrument within 100 km makes no request at all and the app says so.
  • The nearshore wave model: one request, to this app's own file host. Beside the buoys, the app shows what Scripps' nearshore wave model says at a point a few hundred metres off the beach itself. Your device does not contact Scripps for that. The model is read every six hours by the developer, on the developer's own machine, for every point at once, and what your phone downloads is one static file of the result from the app's own file host (fernando-enterprise-llc.github.io) — the same file for every reader, naming no spot in the request, asked for once rather than once per spot. That host is the same one § 4 already names under "Dataset downloads" below; this adds no new destination.
  • The tide, with no signal at all. The app carries NOAA's own published harmonic constants for California's tide stations inside its dataset, so the tide height, whether it is rising or falling, and the next high or low are worked out on your phone, with no network at all. Those constants are read once by the developer when a dataset is built. When you do have a signal the app still asks NOAA for the official predictions (api.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov, already named above) and shows those instead; the computed curve is the fallback and is labelled as computed.
  • Water clarity from the satellite: one request, to this app's own file host. When you open a spear spot, the app asks its own static file host (fernando-enterprise-llc.github.io) for one file holding NOAA CoastWatch's satellite water-clarity values for every spear entry point. It is the same file for every reader, it names no spot in the request, and it is asked for once — never once per spot. Your device does not contact NOAA CoastWatch (coastwatch.noaa.gov) for this. NOAA's satellite grid is read once a night by the developer, on the developer's own machine, and what your phone downloads is the result of that reading — so NOAA's servers see neither you, your device, nor which spot you opened. It used to work the other way: opening a spot sent that spot's coordinates to NOAA CoastWatch, twice, and when that server did not answer the screen said so instead of telling you anything about the water. That host is the same one § 4 already names under "Dataset downloads" below; this adds no new destination.
  • Water quality: one request, to this app's own file host, and only when you switch the layer on. With the Water quality layer on, the app asks its own static file host (fernando-enterprise-llc.github.io) for one file holding the readings seven California coastal counties published. It is the same file for every reader, it names no beach, no station and no county in the request, and it is asked for once — never once per beach and never as you pan. When the layer is off, the app makes no water-quality request at all. It used to ask the California Open Data Portal (data.ca.gov) for the State's bacteria results at the monitoring stations for the beaches on your screen, and that request named those station codes; it does not, and data.ca.gov is no longer a host this app contacts for anything. Your device never contacts a county. The counties are read once a day by the developer, on the developer's own machine, and what your phone downloads is the result of that reading — so no county server sees you, your device or anything you looked at. That host is the same one § 4 already names under "Dataset downloads" below; this adds no new destination. The list of coastal counties and the address each one publishes its readings at still ships inside the app, so opening that list downloads nothing; tapping a county hands its address to your browser, and the paragraph on links you tap below says what that means.
  • Running a trip plan. When you run a trip, the app asks the agencies named above about the places in that trip — the National Weather Service (api.weather.gov) for the forecast and any alerts, NOAA tides and currents (api.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov), the National Data Buoy Center (www.ndbc.noaa.gov) and NOAA CoastWatch (coastwatch.noaa.gov). It contacts no host that is not already named in this section. The trip planner is now the ONLY part of this app that asks NOAA CoastWatch anything: the spear spot pages stopped doing so, as the water-clarity paragraph above describes, and a trip plan is something you press a button to run rather than something that happens when a screen opens. Readings this app collects centrally rather than on your phone — stream gauge flows, for one — arrive with the dataset download described below and cost your device no request at all. Each request the app makes about a PLACE names a place or a station the app already publishes in its dataset — a lake, a zone, a named tide station, a numbered buoy. One request is not like the others, and it is the subject of the paragraph after next: when you plan a trip around a spot of your own rather than around a place this app publishes, the forecast for that spot is asked for by its coordinates, because coordinates are the only way to ask for it. Those are coordinates you placed — a pin you dropped or pasted — and never a reading of where you are; nothing in this app reads your position to build a plan. Those servers therefore see which places, or which spot, you asked about, which is the same shape of disclosure as the map tiles above, at the moment you press the button rather than continuously.
  • What starts a trip plan, and what the planner never reads. A plan begins from a spot you choose, and there are exactly three ways to choose one: a place you pick from this app's own list — a lake, a water, a wildlife area, a national forest, a hunt zone — a pin you drop on this app's own map, or coordinates you paste in from a Google Maps or Apple Maps link. There is no fourth, and the absence is deliberate: the planner never reads your position. It cannot. There is no "use my location" button on it, nothing in it asks the device where you are, and the code that would have to do the asking is banned from the whole planner by a test that reads the source. The one screen in this app that reads your GPS is still "Where am I?" on the Map tab, which you press yourself, which answers on the device, and which is described at the top of this section. So which places the app asks about is a consequence of the spot you chose, and of nothing else. That is a smaller disclosure than the one this paragraph used to describe — where the places followed from where you were standing — and it is your choice throughout: pick a place from the app's own list and the app asks about the place.
  • A pin you drop, paste or pick. When you plan a trip around a spot rather than around a place this app publishes — a pin you drop on the map, or coordinates you paste in from a maps link — that position is kept on this device, inside the saved trip, in this app's own files. It is not sent to the developer, it is not synced, and there is no code path in the app that could send it anywhere else. It goes to exactly one place and only when you run the plan: the National Weather Service (api.weather.gov), to fetch the forecast for that spot. That is the same host § 4 already names for every other trip, and it is told a position rather than a place name — which is a real difference from the rest of this section, and it is your choice to make: pick a place from the app's own list instead, and the app asks about the place rather than about your pin. Deleting the saved trip deletes the pin with it.
  • Dataset downloads. The regulations dataset and these legal pages come from the app's static file host on GitHub Pages (fernando-enterprise-llc.github.io). That includes the files a subscription unlocks. Taking the regulations dataset itself first: The download is the same for everyone and carries nothing about what you bought — every device asks for the same files and receives the same bytes — and whether your device is entitled to Pro is decided on the device from Apple's signed transaction record, after the files have arrived, so the file host is never told which tier you hold, never asked to check one, and has no account, sign-in or token to check it against.

Three smaller files sit beside that dataset and work differently, and it is worth being plain about them rather than leaving you to work it out. CDFW's waterfowl hunt results, CDFW's deer harvest table and the operator directory are bytes only a device that can open a Pro planner screen has any use for, so before asking for them the app decides — on your own device, from the same signed record — whether it can open one, and if it cannot it does not ask for them at all. Which of those three a device asks for is therefore not identical for every reader, and a host that kept request logs could tell the two groups apart from the file names alone. Nothing in any of those requests names you, your device, your purchase or your position, there is no server of the developer's anywhere in that path, and the developer keeps no logs of any of it.

  • Links you tap. If you tap "Open in Apple Maps" or "Open in Google Maps" for a coordinate or a saved waypoint, that coordinate is handed to Apple or Google in the link, and their privacy policies apply from that point. That only ever happens when you tap it. The same is true of a county water-quality map: tapping a county's row, or a beach's county link, opens that county's own page in your browser, and from that point the county's site and its own privacy practices apply. Nothing about you is in the link. Most of these are one address with no query string, no identifier and nothing the app added — the same address for every reader. Two counties publish an address that can open on a place, and for a beach in one of those the link carries that beach: Ventura's carries the beach's own published position out of the State's beach directory that ships inside this app, and Orange's carries the name of one of the county's own six city pages. Both are values this app publishes, both are identical for every reader looking at that beach, and neither is a reading of where you are — nothing in this app puts your position into a link, and nothing in this app reads your position to build one. In every case the app itself never contacts the county.

Every one of those requests carries your device's IP address to the server that answers it, the same as opening any web page. Several of them also carry a line identifying the app and the developer — "CAHuntFishGuide/1.0 (support@fernandoenterprise.com)" or the same in a slightly different form — because the National Weather Service and NOAA require a caller to identify itself and refuse requests that do not. That line names the developer, never you. The developer receives nothing from any of these requests, keeps no logs, runs no server in the path, and has no account to attach anything to. Those servers are run by the agencies and companies named, under their own privacy practices, not the developer's.

5. Notifications

Season reminders, deadline reminders and wallet reminders are local notifications scheduled by the app on your device. There is no push server, no push certificate, and no notification service in between. Nothing about a reminder leaves your phone, and the reminders still work with the phone in airplane mode.

6. Purchases

The optional subscription — CA Hunt & Fish Pro — are sold and billed by Apple through your Apple Account. Apple handles the payment; the developer never sees your payment details, your name, your billing address, or your Apple Account. Which tier your device is entitled to is determined on the device from Apple's own signed transaction record, and whether you are a founding user is determined the same way, from Apple's signed record of when your account first obtained the app. Both of those come from Apple to your device and go no further. Apple's handling of that data is governed by Apple's privacy policy. Refunds are handled by Apple at reportaproblem.apple.com.

7. Do Not Track

This app does not track you over time or across other apps or websites, and no third party collects information about your activity through it. Because the app does not conduct that kind of tracking at all, there is nothing for it to change in response to a browser or system "Do Not Track" signal, and it does not respond to one.

8. Other parties

No. There is no analytics SDK, no advertising SDK, no attribution SDK, no session-replay script, and no third-party tracker of any kind in this app. No other party collects personally identifiable information about your activities over time or across different websites or apps through your use of it.

The servers named in section 4 receive the individual requests described there and nothing more, and they are government agencies and platform providers operating under their own published practices.

9. Health and safety information

The app shows state and federal health-related information about *places* — fish-consumption advisories and safety notices — attributed to the agency that published them, with the date. It also shows beach water-quality readings for the counties that publish them and allow this app's developer to read them automatically, in those counties' own words and numbers, with each county's own date; it shows no water-quality reading of its own, works out no band or conclusion from a county's numbers, and for every other county it links out to the page that county publishes. That information describes a location on the day it was sampled or issued. It is not a determination about you, about your health, or about whether anything is safe to do, and the app makes no such determination. The app collects no health information about anyone.

10. Children

The app is not directed to children. It is rated 13+, its subject is state hunting and fishing regulation, and it has no accounts, no messaging, no user-generated content shared with anyone, and no advertising. It collects no personal information from anyone, of any age, including no transmitted location information.
